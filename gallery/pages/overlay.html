Beta blockers in atrial fibrillation

Revision notes for the cardiology block · reading time 6 minutes

Mechanism

Beta blockers compete with adrenaline and noradrenaline at beta-adrenergic receptors. At the atrioventricular node this slows conduction and lengthens the refractory period, so fewer of the chaotic atrial impulses reach the ventricles and the ventricular rate falls.

Cardioselective agents such as bisoprolol and metoprolol act mainly on beta-1 receptors in the heart. Selectivity is relative rather than absolute: at higher doses they also block beta-2 receptors in the airways, which matters for patients with asthma.

Rate control

Rate control with a beta blocker is the first-line strategy for most stable patients with atrial fibrillation. A resting heart rate below 110 beats per minute is a reasonable initial target when symptoms are mild and left ventricular function is preserved.

When a single agent is not enough, digoxin can be added, particularly in sedentary patients or those with heart failure. Non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers are an alternative but should be avoided when the ejection fraction is reduced.

Cautions

In patients with diabetes, beta blockers can mask the adrenergic warning signs of hypoglycaemia, such as tremor and palpitations, while sweating is usually preserved. Advise patients to check their glucose when they feel unwell.

Abrupt withdrawal after long-term use may cause rebound tachycardia and, in patients with coronary disease, worsening angina. Taper the dose over one to two weeks instead.

Avoid non-selective agents in asthma. If a beta blocker is essential, choose a cardioselective drug at the lowest effective dose and review the patient after the first doses.

Stroke prevention

Rate control does not remove the risk of stroke. Assess every patient with the CHA2DS2-VASc score and weigh it against bleeding risk before deciding on anticoagulation.

Direct oral anticoagulants are preferred over warfarin for most patients without mechanical heart valves or moderate to severe mitral stenosis.

Key points

Beta blockers slow the ventricular rate by acting on the atrioventricular node. Watch for bronchospasm, masked hypoglycaemia and rebound effects on withdrawal, and never let good rate control replace an explicit decision about anticoagulation.
